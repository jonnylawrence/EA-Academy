How Big Things Get Done

Bent Flyvbjerg and Dan Gardner on why major projects fail, and how better planning improves delivery.

Includes IT and software implementation examples.

Core message

Think slow, act fast

⚠️

What usually happens

Teams rush into delivery, discover problems late, then slow down as costs, changes, and rework accumulate. The book describes this failure pattern as “think fast, act slow.”

✅

What successful teams do

They spend much longer clarifying scope, sequencing work, and testing assumptions before moving quickly through execution with fewer surprises.

The problem

The iron law of big projects

Across Flyvbjerg’s large project database, strong performance on cost, schedule, and benefits is exceptionally rare.

0%
Over budget or late
Most large projects miss at least one core target.
0%
On time and on budget
Hitting both targets together is uncommon.
0%
Meet cost, time, and benefits
Only a tiny minority deliver on all three.
The implication for teaching is simple: failure is not random. It follows patterns that can be studied and reduced.

Three ideas

The practical toolkit

🔭

Take the outside view

Forecast using outcomes from similar past projects rather than relying only on internal optimism.

🧱

Build with Lego

Prefer modular, repeatable components so teams learn faster and reduce one-off complexity.

↩️

Think right to left

Start with the end state, then work backward through milestones, dependencies, and decisions.

IT example

How this maps to software delivery

Good translation into IT

Use reference-class data from similar implementations.
Prototype early in sandboxes and test integrations before rollout.
Prefer modular services, APIs, and staged deployment over monolithic rewrites.

Typical failure pattern

Political or executive deadlines force premature launch.
Architecture gets locked in before performance and user pathways are proven.
Integration, data quality, training, and adoption problems are left until late.
Illustrative cases

Healthcare.gov became a widely cited example of rushed launch and late integration failure, while government IT programmes such as Canada’s firearms registry are often cited as examples of severe underestimation and escalation.

Teaching slide

Simple teaching model

Planning effortHigh
Execution speedFast once ready
Late-stage surprisesLow
Teach it as two phases

1. Slow thinking: scope, sequence, options, tests, risk, evidence.

2. Fast action: disciplined delivery against a well-developed plan.

Useful classroom question

Where are we currently mistaking visible activity for genuine progress?

Discussion

Questions for learners

Reflection prompts

  • What evidence would count as an outside view for your next project?
  • Which parts of your work could be made modular?
  • Where does early commitment make later change expensive?

Applied settings

  • Electronic patient record upgrades.
  • Major service redesign or pathway transformation.
  • Data migration, reporting, and governance programmes.
🧠

Memorable takeaway

Projects usually do not fail in the middle. They start wrong, then reveal the consequences later.
